# 21 - Interview Answer Retrieval and Semantic Compression

## Purpose

This rule makes COMPASS interview preparation easier to recall and reconstruct under pressure without weakening accuracy, TruthGuard, or evidence boundaries.

The goal is not verbatim memorization. The goal is to preserve the meaning and evidence of a strong answer in a compact structure the candidate can expand naturally.

## Authority

This rule changes presentation only; it does not create candidate evidence.

Apply, in order:

1. the user's direct current instruction;
2. current verified Source of Truth and user-specific interview policy;
3. `rules/04-truthguard.md` and claim-depth/do-not-claim controls;
4. `rules/20-professional-effectiveness-evidence.md` when behavioral evidence is used;
5. this rule.

Target questions and job descriptions may identify what evidence is useful. They may not create experience, ownership, outcomes, technologies, leadership scope, or professional-effectiveness evidence.

## Recall-First Contract

When COMPASS proposes an answer to a likely interview question, default to this order unless the user asks for another format:

1. **Core** - one sentence with the central answer.
2. **Memory hook** - two to four short semantic cues that can regenerate the answer.
3. **Three beats** - no more than three primary ideas to cover.
4. **Story hook** - when an example is useful, one verified story label plus three or four retrieval cues.
5. **Natural answer** - optional demonstration only, placed last and labeled `Do not memorize verbatim`.

The retrieval structure is primary. Polished prose is secondary.

### Core

Answer the actual question directly. Do not hide the answer behind biography, setup, or a long qualification list.

### Memory hook

Use short semantic cues that preserve the logic, for example:

- `Early -> Durable -> Close`
- `Clarify -> Bound -> Decide`
- `Criteria -> Tradeoffs -> Decision`
- `Problem -> Action -> Result`

Do not force an acronym or mnemonic if it distorts the meaning.

### Three beats

Default to at most three competing top-level ideas. Group supporting detail beneath those ideas rather than producing a long flat list.

### Story hook

When a concrete example is requested or materially useful, select one verified story before generating prose.

Useful story shapes include:

- behavioral: `Problem -> Action or decision -> Result`;
- change/stakeholder: `Change -> People -> Action -> Result`;
- decision/tradeoff: `Constraint -> Options -> Decision -> Consequence`.

Preserve contribution depth, collaborators, implementation stage, measured-versus-observational outcomes, and do-not-claim boundaries. Do not splice unrelated experiences into a synthetic story.

## Question-Shape Routing

### Approach or process

For `How do you approach X?`, prefer:

`Principle -> two or three steps -> close`

### Concrete example

For `Can you give me an example?`, switch directly into one story. Do not repeat the full general philosophy unless the interviewer asks for it again.

### Tradeoff or choice

For `How would you choose between X and Y?`, prefer:

`Criteria -> Tradeoffs -> Decision -> what would change the decision`

The first three items are the primary beats; the last is a concise close.

### Technical or architecture

Preserve the declared interview lens and abstraction level. Compress around the few load-bearing decisions or invariants at that level instead of enumerating every possible component, pattern, or failure mode. If the interviewer zooms in, create a new compact structure for that layer.

## Principle-to-Example Pivot

Preparation should explicitly separate these two modes:

1. general approach;
2. concrete example.

When the interviewer says `give me an example`, the prepared response should begin by selecting one story, such as `A good example would be ...`, and then follow the story hook. The candidate should not try to answer both modes at once.

## Ramble Recovery

Interview preparation may include one short recovery pattern:

`Stop -> Re-anchor -> Core answer -> Stop`

Useful natural transitions include:

- `Let me simplify that.`
- `Let me bring that back to your question.`
- `The short answer is ...`
- `I think I started answering that too broadly. Let me restart.`

These are recovery tools, not required scripts. After the reset, give the Core or remaining beats and stop rather than adding extra examples to compensate.

## Natural Answer Guidance

When a Natural answer is useful:

- place it after the retrieval structure;
- label it `Do not memorize verbatim`;
- use natural spoken language;
- preserve the same evidence and claim boundaries;
- prefer concise wording that leaves room for interviewer follow-up.

A polished paragraph must not displace the recall-first structure merely because it reads better on paper.

## Integration with Interview Preparation Notes

This rule does not replace the eight-section Interview Preparation Notes contract in `rules/06-artifact-rules.md`.

Apply recall-first formatting mainly inside:

- source-backed talking points;
- evidence examples to prepare;
- likely-question or objection preparation;
- interview-specific practice derived from those notes.

When the user explicitly requests another format, follow that instruction while preserving TruthGuard and source boundaries.

## Compression Quality Gate

Before presenting a proposed interview answer, check:

1. **Directness** - the Core answers the question.
2. **Retrievability** - the hook can regenerate the answer without memorized prose.
3. **Boundedness** - no more than three competing primary beats.
4. **Story integrity** - examples are coherent and source-backed.
5. **TruthGuard** - claims remain within verified depth.
6. **Spoken usability** - the candidate can answer naturally from the cues.
7. **Stop point** - it is clear where the answer ends.

If an answer is accurate but hard to retrieve or speak naturally, compress it before release.
